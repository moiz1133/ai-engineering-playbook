# Impact of HNSW on production RAG systems

## Executive Summary
The Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) algorithm is a leading method for approximate nearest neighbor searches, particularly in production RAG systems. While it offers efficient indexing and high recall, challenges arise in terms of latency and performance degradation as dataset sizes increase. HNSW can achieve sub-10ms latency with 95%+ recall at optimized ef_search values, but trade-offs become significant at higher recall levels. Recent case studies illustrate that performance can degrade substantially with larger datasets, with latency increasing significantly as recall targets rise. For instance, recall degradation can occur faster than with exact nearest neighbor searches as the corpus grows, necessitating careful tuning of parameters such as ef_search and m. HNSW is implemented in several vector databases, with varying implications for scalability and efficiency. This report provides quantitative insights and real-world examples to better understand HNSW's impact on production RAG systems.

## What is the HNSW algorithm and how does it perform approximate nearest neighbor search?
The Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) algorithm is a graph-based approach tailored for efficient approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) searches. It leverages a hierarchical structure that facilitates navigation through large datasets, allowing for the identification of the closest items without exhaustive comparisons. This method is particularly effective in managing the complexities associated with high-dimensional data, making it a robust choice for ANN tasks [1][2][3][4].

HNSW's design enables it to maintain high recall rates while providing fast search speeds, which is critical in applications where quick retrieval of similar items is necessary. The algorithm's efficiency stems from its ability to balance the trade-offs between search accuracy and computational resources, making it a preferred option in various production environments [2][3]. Recent benchmarks indicate that HNSW can deliver sub-10ms latency on datasets with millions of vectors while achieving over 95% recall at optimized ef_search values [20]. However, the recall-latency trade-off is entirely controlled by ef_search at query time, allowing for tuning without rebuilding the index [30]. Furthermore, as dataset sizes increase, the performance of HNSW can degrade, particularly when the ef_search parameter is not adjusted accordingly [35].

## Which production vector databases and RAG frameworks use HNSW as their default index?
Weaviate is one of the notable vector databases that incorporates HNSW in its indexing options, specifically within its hfresh index, which combines HNSW for centroid indexing with a focus on memory efficiency. Additionally, Pinecone and Qdrant are recognized as significant vector databases in the field. However, while Pinecone supports HNSW, it does not use it as the default indexing method; instead, it offers multiple indexing options, including its proprietary method [5][6][7][8]. Qdrant has implemented HNSW but has also highlighted the "HNSW scaling paradox," where performance degrades significantly with larger datasets, emphasizing the need for careful configuration [31].

Redis also employs HNSW, emphasizing its growing adoption in production environments [21]. Notably, pgvector has integrated HNSW as a default indexing method, showcasing its effectiveness in PostgreSQL environments for AI workloads [38]. The integration of HNSW within these frameworks highlights its importance in enhancing the performance of vector searches, particularly in systems that require rapid and efficient data retrieval.

## What are the measured latency and recall tradeoffs of HNSW compared to exact nearest neighbor search at production scale?
HNSW presents a notable recall-latency trade-off, particularly as the size of the database increases. Research indicates that recall tends to degrade more rapidly than in exact nearest neighbor searches when using fixed ef_search values. For instance, increasing the recall from 0.80 to 0.95 can lead to a latency increase of approximately 31%, which is generally acceptable; however, the latency can increase 3-5 times when moving from 0.95 to 0.99 recall [20][22]. 

Specific datasets have shown that as the number of documents increases, latency can rise dramatically. For example, one case study reported a latency of 1,129ms at 200,000 documents, which became 12 times slower when the dataset expanded to 2 million documents, despite maintaining acceptable accuracy [31]. Furthermore, a practical use case demonstrated that for fixed HNSW settings, recall degrades faster than flat search as the corpus grows, underscoring the complexities involved in optimizing HNSW for large datasets [35]. This relationship highlights the need for production systems to carefully consider these trade-offs when implementing HNSW to ensure that performance meets operational requirements. Benchmarks show that while HNSW can achieve high recall, the latency implications at higher recall levels necessitate careful tuning for specific workloads [19][20].

## What are the known failure modes or limitations of HNSW in production RAG deployments?
In large-scale RAG systems, HNSW indexing encounters several limitations, particularly concerning latency and recall degradation when datasets exceed ten million records. Performance tends to worsen in comparison to flat search methods under these conditions. Specific failure modes include unexpected drops in recall as the dataset grows, which can lead to significant retrieval inaccuracies if not monitored [22][23]. For instance, one deployment experienced a significant drop in accuracy as the number of documents increased, highlighting the critical need for monitoring and adjustment [31].

Additionally, poorly configured vector operations are common causes of failures in production systems, as they can strain memory allocators and background processes [26]. The tuning of parameters such as ef_search and m is crucial; incorrect settings can lead to performance degradation and increased latency. Case studies have shown that teams often face difficulties when they assume RAG systems are fully resolved after initial demonstrations, leading to performance degradation during actual deployment [21][22]. Moreover, diagnosing silent retrieval degradation in RAG systems with HNSW has been emphasized as a critical concern, as performance changes with scale can go unnoticed without proper monitoring [36]. This emphasizes the importance of ongoing performance evaluation and adjustment in production environments.

## How does HNSW compare to other indexing methods in terms of scalability and efficiency in RAG systems?
HNSW is recognized as one of the top-performing indexing methods for vector similarity searches, providing state-of-the-art performance characterized by fast search speeds and high recall. This efficiency makes it particularly suitable for RAG systems. When compared to other indexing methods, such as Inverted File (IVF), HNSW demonstrates superior scalability and efficiency in data retrieval. For example, benchmarks indicate that HNSW can achieve significantly lower latency compared to IVF while maintaining comparable recall rates [19][27].

However, it is important to note that HNSW is not universally applicable. In scenarios where the dataset is small or when the overhead of maintaining the HNSW structure outweighs its benefits, alternative methods may be more suitable [30]. The advantages of HNSW in terms of performance and scalability position it as a leading choice for organizations looking to implement effective RAG systems, particularly in scenarios where rapid data access and high accuracy are paramount. Organizations must also consider the specific workload characteristics and dataset sizes when selecting an indexing method, as these factors can influence the overall performance and efficiency of the system [28][29].
